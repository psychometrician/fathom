## Why two

The book makes its argument by running code, so nearly every page holds a
working program. Two licenses fall out of that one fact.

A NonCommercial term on the prose leaves a print edition worth publishing,
which is the arrangement every free R book of this kind uses. A permissive
license on the code means a reader can copy an example straight into a real
project, which is the only thing the examples are for. A single license could
not do both.

## What the two licenses do NOT cover

**The output printed on these pages is not all ours, and this book is unusual
in how much of it is not.**

Every report in this book is produced by running the real tool against a real
document at render time — that is the whole point of building it this way, and
[the preface](index.qmd) explains why. Those documents are other people's:
seventeen files from npm, Hacker News, Natural Earth, Synthea, ESPN, SpaceX,
Open-Meteo, Stripe, Wikidata, Peter Norvig's `pytudes`, Microsoft, New York
City, GitHub, Kaggle, OpenLibrary, the FDA and the City of Chicago.

**They keep their own terms.** The repository's `NOTICE` lists them, with the
two groups it distinguishes and the reason for distinguishing them, and each
corpus entry's `NOTES.md` records the source and the day it was fetched. When a
page here prints a field name or a value, that content belongs to whoever made
the document, not to this book.

## One file that is not ours

`apa.csl` is the APA 7th edition citation style from the [Citation Style
Language](https://citationstyles.org/) project, used unchanged under [CC BY-SA
3.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/).

It keeps that license, and it has to: **CC BY-SA forbids adding restrictions to
the work, and NonCommercial is one.** So the file travels under its own terms
wherever this book goes, and anyone reusing it answers to those rather than to
these.
